Freelancer Annual Review: How to Look Back at Your Year (and Plan a Better One)

By Alex Lin · November 17, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Most freelancers never review their year. December arrives, the invoices slow down, and the year just ends — no summary, no lessons, no plan for the next one. Then January starts and the same year happens again: the same slow client, the same under-priced work, the same surprise that the money never quite added up. In the sample, a freelancer earned $48,000 in the year, up from $41,000 the year before — a raise on paper. But the review showed she had worked 210 more hours to earn it. Her hourly rate had actually gone down. Without a review, she would have called it a good year and repeated it. With one, she raised her rate, dropped the client who paid late four times, and planned a year that paid more for fewer hours. An annual review is not a report card. It is two hours, once a year, with your own numbers — and it is the cheapest way to make next year better than this one.

1. Start with the money: what came in, and what it cost you

The review starts with the year's income, counted properly. Add up everything you were actually paid — not invoiced, paid — month by month. In the sample, the freelancer's paid total was $48,000, and the monthly spread showed three months under $2,500 and one month over $6,000: the year was not steady, it was spiky, and the spikes were doing all the work. Then count the hours. Not the hours you remember — the hours your tracker and calendar say you worked, including the admin, the emails, and the revisions nobody paid for. Divide the money by the hours and you have your real hourly rate for the year, which is the number that matters. In the sample it was lower than the year before, even though the income was higher. That one number changes the whole conversation: you did not earn more, you worked more. Write the two numbers down — total paid, real hourly rate — and keep them next to each other. Everything else in the review is about moving the second number up without breaking the first.

2. Sort your clients: who paid well, who paid late, who cost you

Next, list every client you worked for this year, with three numbers each: how much they paid you, how many hours they took, and how they paid. In the sample, 3 clients brought in 62% of the income — and one of them paid late 4 times, each time costing a chase and a week of worry. The sorting is the point: the clients at the top of the list are the ones to keep and grow, the ones at the bottom are the ones to raise, retrain, or release. A client who pays $68 an hour and answers in a day is worth more than a client who pays $75 and takes three weeks to approve anything, because the second one eats hours you never bill. Mark each client in one of three columns — keep, raise, release — and be honest. In the sample, the freelancer marked 5 projects to drop and 2 to double, and the year's income did not fall: the dropped work was the work that paid least per hour. A client list you never sort is a client list that sorts you.

3. Find the work that paid best per hour — and the work that didn't

Income by client is only half the picture. The other half is income by kind of work. In the sample, the freelancer's rate averaged $55 an hour at the start of the year and $68 by the end — but the lowest-paying client still took 18% of the hours, at the old rate, doing the work she liked least. Break the year into types of work — the service, the deliverable, the client type — and work out what each earned per hour. The pattern is almost always the same: one or two kinds of work pay far better than the rest, and one kind pays badly and takes forever. That is your plan for next year in one line: do more of the first, less of the second. In the sample, the freelancer found that short strategy calls paid twice as much per hour as long production work, and she rebuilt her offer around them. You do not need a new skill to earn more next year. You usually need to sell more of the skill you already have that pays best.

4. Count the leaks: the hours and money that quietly disappeared

Every year has leaks, and the review is where you find them. Go through the year's records and count the hours you worked but never billed — the extra revision, the "quick call" that ran an hour, the scope that grew without a new quote. In the sample, the freelancer found 11 unpaid or under-billed hours in the year's records, worth $748 at the new rate — money she had already earned and simply never asked for. Then count the money leaks: subscriptions you forgot, tools you pay for and never open, a late fee you never charged, a discount you gave and never wrote down. Small leaks look harmless one at a time; added up over a year they are a month's rent. Write each leak down with its amount, and next to it the fix — a scope clause, a calendar block, a cancelled subscription. A leak you write down is a leak you can close. A leak you never look for just happens again next year.

5. Look at the year as a calendar: when were you busy, when were you broke

A year is not one number, it is twelve months, and the shape of it matters as much as the total. Plot the year's income month by month, and next to it the hours. In the sample, the freelancer's three slowest months were all in the same quarter, and the busiest month was the one right after — the year had a season, and she had never noticed it. Once you see the season, you can plan for it: save in the fat months for the thin ones, book retainer work before the slow quarter, take the holiday in the month that is always quiet instead of the month that is always busy. The calendar also shows the personal cost: in the sample, the busiest month was also the month she worked six weekends in a row, and the review was the first time she had admitted it. A year that pays well and burns you out is not a good year. The calendar is where you decide what next year's shape should be — not just how much money, but when, and at what pace.

6. Write down what actually worked — and what you will stop doing

Numbers tell you what happened. The review also needs a short, honest list of what worked and what did not. In the sample, the freelancer wrote down five things that worked — the retainer client, the rate rise in June, the template that cut invoicing to two minutes, the referral ask she finally made, the morning she blocked for deep work — and five things that did not: the client who paid late, the discount she regretted, the project she took out of guilt, the tool she bought and never used, the month she said yes to everything. The rule is simple: the "worked" list is next year's plan, and the "did not" list is next year's stop-doing list. Do not write a list of twenty. Five and five is enough, and it fits on one page. Tape that page where you work. In January, when a familiar bad offer arrives, the list is the thing that reminds you that you already decided.

7. Turn the review into next year's plan: three numbers and three decisions

A review that ends in a drawer is a diary. The last step is to turn it into a plan you can hold in one hand: three numbers and three decisions. In the sample, the freelancer's three numbers were a target income of $56,000, a real hourly rate of $75, and no more than 1,600 billable hours — more money, a higher rate, fewer hours, all written down together so one cannot grow by eating the others. Her three decisions were to raise the lowest-paying client or release them, to sell more of the work that paid best per hour, and to close the leaks the review had found. Two hours, one afternoon, a notebook, and the year's invoices — that was the whole review. She did not need a new tool, a new course, or a new skill. She needed to look at the year she had already lived, and decide — on purpose — what the next one would be. Do the review before the year ends, while the numbers are still warm and the lessons are still honest. Next year's freelancer will thank you for it.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer annual review checklist

  1. Add up what you were actually paid this year, month by month, and divide it by the hours you really worked: in the sample, $48,000 looked like a raise until the review showed 210 more hours — the real hourly rate had gone down, and that changed everything.
  2. List every client with what they paid, how many hours they took, and how they paid: in the sample, 3 clients brought in 62% of the income, and one of them paid late 4 times — sort them into keep, raise, and release, and be honest.
  3. Break the year into kinds of work and find what paid best per hour: in the sample, the rate rose from $55 to $68, but the lowest-paying client still took 18% of the hours — do more of the work that pays best, less of the rest.
  4. Count the leaks — hours worked but never billed, and money that quietly disappeared: in the sample, 11 unbilled hours were worth $748 at the new rate, plus forgotten subscriptions and a discount never written down.
  5. Plot income and hours month by month and read the season: in the sample, three slow months sat in the same quarter and the busiest month cost six weekends in a row — plan the slow quarter in advance and take the holiday in the quiet month.
  6. Write five things that worked and five that did not: in the sample, the retainer, the June rate rise, and the two-minute invoice template made the first list — the late-paying client and the guilt project made the second — and the second list became next year's stop-doing list.
  7. Turn the review into three numbers and three decisions: in the sample, $56,000 income, a $75 real hourly rate, and no more than 1,600 billable hours — plus raise-or-release the lowest client, sell more of the best-paying work, and close the leaks — all on one page, taped where you work.
