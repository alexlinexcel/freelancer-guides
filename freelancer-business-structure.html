Freelancer Business Structure: LLC vs Sole Proprietor (and When to Switch)

By Alex Lin · November 16, 2026 · Freelancing guides

When you start freelancing, you are a sole proprietor by default — the moment you take your first paid job, that is what you are, no paperwork required. An LLC is the upgrade most freelancers hear about sooner or later: a separate legal entity that sits between you and the world. Both can work. The right choice depends on how much you earn, how much risk your work carries, and how much paperwork you are willing to do. Here is how to decide — and when to switch.

1. A sole proprietor is you, and an LLC is a shield

As a sole proprietor, there is no legal wall between you and your business: if a client sues, they sue you, and your personal savings, your car, and your home are all in the picture. An LLC — a limited liability company — creates a separate legal person: the LLC signs the contract, the LLC takes the risk, and your personal assets are behind a wall. In the sample, a freelancer who took a $12,000 contract with a big client was asked to carry liability insurance before signing — the client's lawyers wanted someone to sue besides a person. The wall is not magic (it fails if you mix personal and business money), but it is real, and it is the main reason freelancers form LLCs.

2. The cost gap is real, but smaller than people think

A sole proprietorship costs nothing to start — you already are one. An LLC costs money to form and money to keep: in the sample, a freelancer paid a $500–$800 one-time filing fee (varies by state) plus a $50–$300 annual state fee, every year, whether she earned anything or not. That sounds like a lot until you compare it with one lost dispute: a single client claim can cost thousands in legal fees before anyone wins anything. The question is not "is an LLC free" — it is "is the wall worth a few hundred dollars a year at my income level". For a freelancer earning $40,000 a year on low-risk writing work, the honest answer is often "not yet".

3. Taxes: the part everyone gets wrong

Here is the surprise: by default, a single-member LLC is taxed exactly like a sole proprietorship — the IRS ignores the LLC and taxes the profit on your personal return, same forms, same rates. The LLC does not save you tax by itself. What saves tax is the election that comes later: once your profit is high enough, an LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corporation, which can reduce self-employment tax on part of your income. In the sample, a freelancer who crossed $60,000 in annual profit talked to her accountant about the S-corp election — below that level, the extra payroll and bookkeeping costs ate the savings. Do not form an LLC expecting a tax cut on day one; form it for the wall, and let the tax decision come later, with a professional.

4. Clients and credibility: the quiet benefit

Some clients do not care about your structure. Some care a lot. In the sample, a freelancer who added "LLC" to her invoices and contracts noticed that bigger clients — the ones with procurement departments and vendor forms — stopped asking awkward questions about who they were paying. A company name on the contract looks like a business, not a side hobby, and procurement systems are built to pay companies. It is not the reason to form an LLC, but it is a real side effect: the letters after your name signal that you take the business seriously, and serious clients notice.

5. The paperwork you are actually signing up for

A sole proprietorship asks almost nothing of you: track income, track expenses, pay quarterly estimated tax, file a Schedule C. An LLC asks for a little more, every year: an annual report to the state, a separate bank account you actually use, minutes or records for big decisions, and a registered agent address in some states. In the sample, a freelancer who formed an LLC and then kept paying business bills from her personal card discovered the hard way that the wall only works if you respect it — commingling money is the fastest way a court ignores the LLC entirely. The paperwork is not heavy, but it is permanent: if you hate admin, the sole proprietorship's simplicity is a genuine feature.

6. When to stay a sole proprietor

Stay put if your income is modest, your work is low-risk, and your clients are small businesses or individuals who pay by invoice without vendor forms. In the sample, a freelancer earning $40,000 a year writing blog posts and newsletters stayed a sole proprietor for three years — no employees, no office lease, no client data more sensitive than a Google Doc — and put the money she would have spent on LLC fees into her tax savings instead. The rule is simple: if a lawsuit against you would have nothing to take and nobody to impress, the wall is solving a problem you do not have yet. You can always form the LLC later.

7. When to switch — the five triggers

Switch when any of these show up: your annual profit passes roughly $50,000–$60,000 and the S-corp conversation becomes worth having; a client requires you to be an LLC or carry liability insurance; you hire subcontractors or employees; you sign a lease, take on debt, or handle sensitive client data; or you simply sleep better knowing the wall is there. In the sample, a freelancer switched the month she signed her first $12,000 contract with a company that required insurance — the trigger was the client, not the calendar. Forming an LLC takes days, not months, so there is no prize for doing it early; the prize is doing it at the moment the risk arrives.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer business-structure checklist

  1. Check your income: if you earn under about $40,000 a year on low-risk work, a sole proprietorship is usually fine for now.
  2. List your risks: client data, contracts, leases, employees — if any are serious, the LLC wall starts to matter.
  3. Price the LLC in your state: one-time filing fee plus the annual fee, every year, before you decide.
  4. Open a separate business bank account either way — even as a sole proprietor, mixing money is how trouble starts.
  5. Set aside tax money from every payment (roughly a quarter of profit is a common starting point) so the first tax bill is not a surprise.
  6. Ask one client-facing question: do any of your clients require an LLC, insurance, or a vendor form? That answer decides the timing.
  7. Before filing anything, talk to a local accountant or attorney for an hour — the rules, fees, and tax elections vary by state, and one hour of advice beats a year of guessing.
