Freelancer Hourly vs Project Pricing: How to Choose (and When to Switch)

By Alex Lin · November 15, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Every freelancer hits the same question, usually right after a client asks "what do you charge?" — hourly, or per project? The wrong answer costs you money either way: hourly can punish you for working fast, and project pricing can punish you for a client who keeps changing their mind. Here is how to choose, how to price each one fairly, and when to switch.

1. The real question is who carries the risk

Hourly and project pricing are not two prices for the same work — they are two different deals about risk. Hourly means the client carries the risk: if the work takes longer, they pay more, and you get paid for every hour you work. Project pricing means you carry the risk: if the work takes longer, you earn less per hour, and if it takes less, you earn more. That is the whole decision in one sentence. When the scope is fuzzy, the client is new, or the work depends on other people, hourly protects you. When the scope is clear, you know the work well, and you can finish it faster than average, project pricing pays you for that speed. Pick the side that matches who can control the outcome.

2. Hourly pricing: when it protects you

Charge hourly when the work is open-ended: research, ongoing support, maintenance, or any job where the client will keep adding requests. Hourly is also right for a new client you have not worked with yet, because you do not know how slow their feedback will be or how many revisions they will ask for. In the sample, a freelancer with a $50/hour rate took an open-ended website cleanup job hourly; the client's "one small change" requests added 12 hours over a month, and every one of those hours was paid. On a fixed price, those 12 hours would have been free work. Hourly also makes invoicing simple: track the hours, send the invoice, get paid. The downside is the ceiling — you can never earn more than hours times rate, and the faster you get, the less you earn for the same result.

3. Project pricing: when it pays you more

Charge per project when the deliverable is clear and you can estimate the work: a logo package, a five-page website, a monthly bookkeeping close. Project pricing pays you for the result, not the clock, so the better and faster you get, the more you earn per hour. In the sample, a $2,000 project took 25 hours — $80 an hour effective, well above the same freelancer's $50 hourly rate, because they had done the same kind of work a dozen times and knew every shortcut. The catch is scope: a fixed price only works if the scope is written down — what is included, how many revisions, and what counts as extra. Without that, "just one more thing" eats the whole profit. Never agree a project price on a vague brief.

4. The math: how to price a project from your hourly rate

Start from your hourly rate, then add the risk you are taking on. The formula is simple: estimated hours × hourly rate, then add a buffer of 20–30% for the unknowns. In the sample, a project estimated at 20 hours × $50/hour = $1,000, plus a 25% buffer = $1,250. That buffer is not padding — it is the price of the risk you carry when the scope shifts or the client is slow. If the client balks at the number, do not cut the buffer first; cut the scope instead. Fewer pages, fewer revisions, a smaller deliverable — the price follows the scope down, and your rate stays intact. And always ask for a deposit: 50% up front is standard, and it turns a project price from a promise into a commitment on both sides.

5. The hybrid: hourly with a cap, or project with a scope line

You do not have to pick one forever — most experienced freelancers use both, and a hybrid often fits best. Hourly with a cap: you bill hourly, but agree the total will not pass a set number without the client's approval — the client gets cost control, you get paid for every hour. Project with a scope line: a fixed price for a written scope, plus your hourly rate for anything outside it — the client gets a predictable price, you get paid for the extras. In the sample, a freelancer quoted a $1,500 website build with a written scope and a $50/hour rate for extra pages; the client added two pages, paid $300 more, and nobody argued, because the rule was agreed before the work started. Hybrids work because they put the risk where it belongs: the fixed part covers what is known, the hourly part covers what is not.

6. When to switch from one to the other

Switch from hourly to project pricing when you have done the same work enough times to estimate it well — usually after three or four similar jobs, when your estimates stop surprising you. Switch from project back to hourly when the scope starts moving: a client who revises endlessly, a project that depends on a third party, or work where the brief keeps changing. A good rule: if you cannot write the scope in one paragraph, do not fix the price. In the sample, a freelancer moved their standard landing-page job from hourly to a $900 project price after the fourth one, because the hours had settled at 15–18 every time — and kept hourly for custom work, where every job was different. The switch is not a promotion; it is a tool you pick up when the work is predictable enough to price.

7. The decision rule: match the pricing to the work, not the client

There is no better pricing model, only the better one for this job. Clear scope, known work, you are fast at it: project price, with a buffer and a deposit. Fuzzy scope, new client, open-ended work: hourly, with tracked hours and regular invoices. A bit of both: a hybrid with a written scope line. Revisit the choice on every new job — the model that fit the last client may not fit the next one — and track what you actually earn per hour on project work, so you know whether the fixed price is paying you or costing you. In the sample, the freelancer who tracked effective hourly rate on every project found their project work averaged $72 an hour against a $50 hourly rate — proof the switch was worth it, and the number to watch if it ever drops below the hourly rate.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer pricing-model checklist

  1. Ask who carries the risk: fuzzy scope or new client means hourly protects you; clear scope you know well means project pricing can pay more.
  2. Charge hourly for open-ended work, new clients, and anything where the client will keep adding requests.
  3. Charge per project for clear deliverables you can estimate — and write the scope down first, including revisions.
  4. Price a project as estimated hours × your hourly rate, plus a 20–30% buffer for the risk you carry.
  5. Ask for a 50% deposit on project work, and cut scope — not your rate — if the client needs a lower price.
  6. Use a hybrid when it fits: hourly with a cap, or a fixed price with your hourly rate for anything outside the written scope.
  7. Track your effective hourly rate on every project; if it drops below your hourly rate, the fixed price is costing you — switch back.
