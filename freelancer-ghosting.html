Freelancer Ghosting: What to Do When a Client Goes Silent Mid-Project (and How to Protect Your Time and Your Pay)

By Alex Lin · November 17, 2026 · Freelancing guides

You sent the first draft on Tuesday. It is now Monday, and the client has not replied to your email, your message, or your second email. The work is half done, the second payment is not due until the client approves the draft, and your calendar has a hole where this project was supposed to be. Every freelancer meets this silence eventually. In the sample, a $2,400 website redesign with 50% paid upfront ($1,200) went quiet for 6 days right after the first draft was sent — and the freelancer's first instinct was the wrong one: keep working, keep polishing, keep hoping the reply arrives. Ghosting is not a communication problem. It is a cash-flow problem wearing a communication problem's clothes, and the fix is a sequence, not a hope.

1. Busy is not gone: how to tell the difference

Most silence is not ghosting. Clients go on holiday, get pulled into a launch, lose your email in a busy inbox, or wait for their own boss to approve your draft. A busy client replies late; a gone client never replies at all. The difference is the pattern, not the clock. If the client answered quickly before and has now missed one deadline, that is busy. If the client has missed your email, your message, and a nudge on a second channel, and a week has passed with no sign of life, that is a different thing. In the sample, 3 of 5 silent clients came back within 2 weeks once the pause notice named a restart date — they were busy, not gone, and the pause notice was what pulled them back. Do not diagnose the silence in the first 48 hours. Do diagnose it after a week of nothing on every channel.

2. Stop working, don't chase with more work

The most expensive mistake is the obvious one: keep working while the client is silent. You polish the draft, build the next page, answer questions nobody asked — and every hour is unpaid work on a project that may already be dead. Pause the moment the client misses the reply you need to continue. You are not abandoning the project; you are protecting the hours you have already spent. In the sample, the deposit covered the 18 hours already worked, and the unpaid balance was $900 for work delivered but not yet approved — the loss was capped the day the work stopped, and every hour after that would have been free. Tell the client you have paused, in writing, and tell them what restarts the work: their reply, and the next milestone payment. Silence does not earn more of your time.

3. The 4-message follow-up sequence (over 14 days, then stop)

One desperate flood of messages makes you look anxious, and anxious freelancers get ignored. Send four messages, each with a job, and then stop. Day 2: a short nudge — "Just checking you saw the draft, happy to walk through it on a quick call." Day 5: a check-in with one clear question the client can answer in one line — "Do you want the homepage to stay as drafted, or should I move the pricing section up?" Day 9: a pause notice with a date — "I'll pause the project on Friday and hold your slot until the 20th; replying before then restarts the work." Day 14: a close-out email — "I'll close the project for now; the draft and the deposit stay as agreed, and I'm here whenever you're ready to restart." In the sample, the sequence was 4 messages over 14 days and no more than one message per step, and the pause notice on day 9 was the message that got the most replies. Four messages is professional. Fourteen is not.

4. Protect the money: the deposit, the milestones, and the unpaid balance

A ghosted project is a money problem first. If you took 50% upfront, the deposit is yours for the work already done — say so in the close-out email, plainly, so there is no argument later. If the client owes a milestone payment for work already delivered, invoice it now, while the paper trail is fresh, even if you expect silence: an invoice sent is a debt recorded, and an invoice never sent is a favour. In the sample, the unpaid balance was $900 for work delivered but not yet approved, and invoicing it on day 9 meant the client who came back on day 12 paid it without argument — the invoice was already there, dated, itemised, and impossible to dispute. Never let the next milestone be the only thing standing between you and the money you have already earned.

5. Protect the work: don't hand over the keys before you're paid

A silent client who suddenly reappears asking for the source files is not always a returning client — sometimes it is a client collecting the work for free. Do not send editable source files, passwords, or admin access until the milestone they belong to is paid. A watermarked preview, a PDF, or a staging link is enough for a client to approve the work; the working files are the delivery, and delivery happens on payment. In the sample, the freelancer sent a watermarked preview for approval and kept the editable files until the second milestone cleared — and when the client finally replied, the payment arrived first and the files followed, in that order. Your work is your leverage. Do not give it away to someone who has stopped answering you.

6. Close it cleanly: the email that leaves the door open

A ghosted project that never ends is a project that keeps taking up space in your head. Close it, in writing, on day 14. Thank the client, state what was delivered and what was paid, state what happens if they come back — the project restarts at your current rate, with a new timeline, and the next milestone is due before work resumes — and wish them well. In the sample, the close-out email said exactly that, and one of the two clients who never came back referred a colleague six months later — because the last message from the freelancer was professional, not bitter. A clean close protects your reputation and your calendar at the same time, and it is the message that makes a returning client easy to say yes to.

7. Make the next project ghost-proof before it starts

You cannot stop clients going silent, but you can stop silence from costing you money. Put a silence clause in your agreement: if the client does not respond within 10 business days, the project pauses and the next milestone falls due on restart. Take 50% upfront on every project, and split the rest into milestones small enough that no single silence costs you more than a week of work. And agree the approval window up front — "feedback within 5 business days" — so silence has a date attached to it from day one. In the sample, the silence clause was used once, and the client replied within 48 hours of the pause notice — because the clause made the cost of silence visible. The next project does not need a better client. It needs a better agreement.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer ghosting checklist

  1. Wait 48 hours before you diagnose the silence: in the sample, a client who answered quickly before and then missed one deadline was busy, not gone — the pattern across a week, not the clock, is what tells you.
  2. Pause the work the moment the client's reply is what you need to continue: in the sample, the deposit covered the 18 hours already worked, and stopping on day 6 capped the loss — every hour after that would have been free.
  3. Send the 4-message sequence over 14 days, one message per step: in the sample, a day-2 nudge, a day-5 check-in with one clear question, a day-9 pause notice with a date, and a day-14 close-out email — and the day-9 pause notice got the most replies.
  4. Invoice the unpaid balance now, while the paper trail is fresh: in the sample, the $900 for work delivered but not yet approved was invoiced on day 9, and the client who came back on day 12 paid it without argument.
  5. Hold the source files until the milestone is paid: in the sample, a watermarked preview was enough for approval, and the editable files followed only after the second milestone cleared.
  6. Close the project in writing on day 14: in the sample, the close-out email stated what was delivered, what was paid, and that a restart happens at the current rate with the next milestone due first — and a client who never came back still referred a colleague six months later.
  7. Ghost-proof the next project before it starts: in the sample, a silence clause said the project pauses after 10 business days without a reply and the next milestone falls due on restart — and the one time it was used, the client replied within 48 hours.
