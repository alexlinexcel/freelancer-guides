Freelancer Contracts: 7 Clauses That Protect Your Time, Your Work, and Your Pay

By Alex Lin · November 15, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Most freelancers start work on a handshake and a friendly email. That works until the day it doesn't: the client who "forgot" the budget, the project that quietly triples in size, the invoice that sits unpaid for three months. A contract is not about distrust. It is the one document that turns a friendly agreement into something you can point at. Here are the 7 clauses every freelancer contract needs, in plain language, with the mistake each one prevents.

1. Scope of work: what you will do — and what you won't

Scope is the most important clause in the whole contract, because almost every freelance dispute is a scope dispute in disguise. Write down the deliverables in concrete terms: how many pages, how many designs, which files, in what format. Then write down what is not included. In the sample, a $3,000 sample project defined as "10-page website, copy provided by the client, two revision rounds" stayed a $3,000 project; the same job described as "build the website" grew into copywriting, photo sourcing, and a third redesign — all unpaid. If a deliverable is not on the list, it is a new job with a new price. The list is the protection.

2. Payment terms: how much, when, and what happens if it's late

State the total fee, the deposit, the payment schedule, and the due date of every invoice. A 50% upfront sample deposit before work starts is the industry norm for a reason: it filters out clients who were never going to pay, and it means you are never more than half out of pocket. In the sample, a freelancer with 14-day sample payment terms and a late fee of 1.5% per month got paid on time far more often than one whose contract said only "payment on completion" — a phrase with no date in it is not a term, it is a wish. Add the consequence in writing: work pauses if an invoice is more than 14 days overdue. You will rarely need to use it. The clause exists so you never have to argue about it.

3. Revision limits: how many rounds, and what counts as a revision

Unlimited revisions are how a two-week project becomes a two-month project. The contract should name the number of revision rounds included — 2 revision rounds is a common starting point — and define what a revision is: changes to work already delivered, not new work. In the sample, a freelancer whose contract said "two rounds of revisions on the delivered draft; new sections and new features are billed separately" finished on schedule; a colleague with no revision clause was still making "small tweaks" six weeks after delivery. Every extra round after the limit is billed at your hourly rate. Clients respect a boundary they can read.

4. Kill fee: what you get paid if the project is cancelled

Projects get cancelled. Budgets vanish, managers change, priorities shift — none of it is your fault, but all of it can leave you unpaid for work already done. A kill fee clause sets the price of cancellation in advance: typically the deposit plus payment for all work completed to date, or a fixed percentage of the total fee. In the sample, a freelancer whose contract said "cancellation after work starts: 50% of the remaining fee, plus payment for all completed work" lost a project on a Tuesday and was still paid for it; a freelancer with no kill fee lost the same kind of project and was paid nothing for two weeks of work. Agree the number when everyone is friendly. That is the only moment it is easy.

5. Ownership and usage: who owns the work, and when

A common trap: the client assumes they own everything the moment you start, and you assume they own it when they pay. The contract should say plainly that ownership transfers on full payment — not before. Until the final invoice clears, the work is yours. In the sample, a freelancer who kept ownership until payment was able to stop a client from launching unpaid work, and was paid within a week; the version of the story without the clause ends with the work live on the client's site and the invoice still unpaid. If the client wants a licence instead of full ownership, price that separately and write down exactly what they may do with the work.

6. Timeline and dependencies: what you owe, and what the client owes you

A deadline is only fair if both sides have deadlines. The contract should name your delivery dates and the client's obligations: feedback within a set number of days, content and logins provided by a set date. In the sample, a freelancer whose contract said "client feedback within 5 business days; delays push the delivery date by the same number of days" never had to explain a late delivery again — the calendar did the explaining. Without the clause, a client who answers after three weeks still expects the original deadline, and you end up working nights to cover their delay. The clause is not confrontational. It is a calendar both sides can see.

7. Termination and dispute handling: how either side walks away cleanly

Every contract should say how it ends. Either side may terminate with written notice; the freelancer is paid for all work completed to the termination date, plus the kill fee if the contract has one. Add one line on disputes: which law applies, and whether you will try mediation before court. In the sample, a freelancer whose contract named a 14-day written-notice termination and payment for completed work ended a bad client relationship with one email and one final invoice; without the clause, the same ending took three weeks of argument about what was owed. A clean exit clause is the reason you can take the job in the first place.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer contract checklist

  1. Write the scope in concrete deliverables — counts, formats, and what is not included.
  2. Set the fee, the 50% upfront sample deposit, the payment schedule, and 14-day sample payment terms with a late fee.
  3. Name the revision rounds included (2 is a common starting point) and define what counts as a revision.
  4. Add a kill fee for cancellation after work starts — deposit plus payment for completed work.
  5. State that ownership transfers on full payment, not before.
  6. Give both sides deadlines: your delivery dates and the client's feedback and content dates, with slippage rules.
  7. Add a clean termination clause — written notice, payment for completed work — and the law that applies.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Adapt it to your local law, and get a lawyer to review anything high-stakes.
