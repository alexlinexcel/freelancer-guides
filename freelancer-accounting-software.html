Freelancer Accounting Software: QuickBooks vs Wave vs FreshBooks (and When a Spreadsheet Wins)

By Alex Lin · November 15, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Every freelancer reaches the same fork in the road: the spreadsheet that worked fine at five clients a month starts creaking at twenty, and someone says "just get QuickBooks." But which software actually fits a one-person business — and is paid software even the right answer yet? Here is a plain comparison of the three names freelancers hear most, plus the honest case for staying on a spreadsheet a little longer.

1. Start with what you actually need, not what the ads sell

Before comparing features, list your real jobs: send invoices, track who paid, record expenses, set aside tax, and see profit at a glance. That is the whole list for most solo freelancers. If you also need payroll, inventory, or multi-currency billing, you are already past the simple tier. In the sample, a freelancer earning about $3,000 a month found that invoicing, expense tracking, and a tax set-aside covered 95% of their bookkeeping — everything else in the software was decoration they paid for and never opened. Write your list first; the software that matches it wins.

2. QuickBooks Online: the most powerful, and the most to learn

QuickBooks is the default answer for a reason: it does everything — invoicing, expenses, bank feeds, reports, tax categories, and it plugs into almost every other tool a freelancer uses. An accountant will almost certainly know it, which matters at tax time. The trade-off is complexity and price: paid plans run roughly $25–$35 a month and up, and the interface carries a lot of small-business machinery a solo freelancer may never need. Choose QuickBooks if you want one tool that grows with you into hiring, payroll, and a CPA relationship — and if you are willing to spend an afternoon learning it.

3. Wave: the free option that covers the basics well

Wave is genuinely free for invoicing and accounting — no trial clock, no card required — which makes it the easiest first step off a spreadsheet. It handles invoices, expenses, receipts, and simple reports cleanly, and its interface is calmer than QuickBooks. The limits show up later: no built-in time tracking, fewer automations, and paid add-ons for payment processing and payroll. In the sample, a freelancer who billed about $3,000 a month used Wave for two years and paid nothing for the software itself, only the card-processing fees when clients paid online. Choose Wave if your budget is zero and your needs are the basic four: invoice, track, record, report.

4. FreshBooks: the friendliest invoicing-first choice

FreshBooks is built around the invoice: beautiful invoices, automatic reminders, late fees, and time tracking baked in, with a client portal that makes you look polished. It is the easiest of the three to live in day to day. The catch is that its accounting depth is lighter than QuickBooks — the double-entry bookkeeping and reporting are simpler, and higher tiers cost more as client counts grow. Choose FreshBooks if invoicing and getting paid on time is your biggest headache and you want the tool to chase late payments for you.

5. When a spreadsheet still wins

Software is not automatically better. If you have under a dozen clients, simple expenses, and no employees, a good spreadsheet is faster, cheaper, and fully yours: no subscription, no export lock-in, no feature you did not ask for. A workbook with income, expenses, tax set-aside, and a monthly dashboard answers the same four questions as the software — and you can change it in five minutes when your business changes. In the sample, a freelancer kept a spreadsheet until monthly income passed about $4,000 and client count passed fifteen; only then did the bank-feed automation of paid software save more time than it cost. The spreadsheet is not the amateur option. It is the right tool until the volume says otherwise.

6. The switching cost nobody mentions

Moving to accounting software is not just the monthly fee — it is the migration. Old invoices, client lists, expense history, and tax categories all need to move, and the first month is always messier than the demo. Before you switch, export everything from your current setup and test the import with one month of data, not a whole year. In the sample, a freelancer who tested with a single month found that half their expense categories did not map cleanly — and fixed the mapping in an afternoon instead of discovering the mess at tax time. Whatever you choose, keep a full export of your data every quarter. Your books should never be held hostage by a subscription.

7. The decision rule: match the tool to the stage you are in

There is no best accounting software, only the best one for your stage. Just starting out, few clients, tight budget: a spreadsheet, or Wave if you want invoices that look professional. Growing steadily, invoicing is the bottleneck: FreshBooks. Scaling up, hiring help, working with an accountant: QuickBooks. And revisit the choice once a year — the tool that fit at $2,000 a month may not fit at $8,000, and the one that felt expensive at the start may be cheap once it saves you five hours a month. In the sample, the freelancer who re-checked yearly spent roughly $25 a month on software that saved about 4 hours of admin — a trade that paid for itself many times over.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer accounting-software checklist

  1. List the jobs you actually need: invoice, track payments, record expenses, set aside tax.
  2. Compare QuickBooks (most powerful), Wave (free basics), and FreshBooks (friendliest invoicing) against that list.
  3. If you have few clients and simple expenses, a spreadsheet still wins — no subscription needed.
  4. Check the current price on the vendor's site before you buy; ranges here are approximate.
  5. Test the switch with one month of data before migrating a whole year.
  6. Export a full copy of your books every quarter, whatever tool you use.
  7. Revisit the choice once a year as your income and client count grow.
