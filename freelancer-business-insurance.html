Freelancer Business Insurance: What You Actually Need (and What You Can Skip)

By Alex Lin · November 15, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Most freelancers never think about insurance until a client asks for it — usually in a contract, usually the week before the work starts, and usually with a number attached: "Contractor must carry $1M in professional liability coverage." That is a bad time to learn what insurance you need. The good news is that most one-person freelance businesses need only one or two policies, and they cost less than most people expect. Here are seven moves to get covered properly — without paying for coverage you will never use.

1. Start with professional liability — it is the one clients actually ask for

Professional liability insurance (also called errors and omissions, or E&O) covers you when a client claims your work caused them a financial loss: a mistake in a spreadsheet, a missed deadline that cost them a launch, advice that turned out wrong. This is the policy clients ask for by name, and the one that unlocks bigger contracts. In the sample, a freelancer who quoted on a corporate project was asked for $1M in professional liability before the work could start — without it, she lost the project to someone who had it. If you buy one policy, buy this one.

2. General liability is cheap — buy it if you ever meet clients in person

General liability covers the physical stuff: a client trips over your laptop bag in a coffee shop, you spill coffee on a client's laptop, your equipment damages something at a client's office. If you work entirely from home and never meet clients face to face, you may not need it. If you ever work on-site, it is usually the cheapest policy you will buy — often bundled with professional liability for a small extra amount. In the sample, a freelancer who added general liability to her professional policy paid only a few dollars more a month for the bundle. Cheap peace of mind, if you leave the house for work.

3. Your home or renter's insurance probably does not cover your business gear

Here is the gap most freelancers miss: a standard home or renter's policy often excludes business equipment, or caps it at a low amount — sometimes as little as $2,500 total. Your laptop, monitor, and external drives can easily be worth more than that. Check your policy, and if the business-equipment limit is low, add a rider or a small business property policy. In the sample, a freelancer whose laptop was stolen from her car found her renter's policy covered only $1,500 of business gear — the laptop alone cost $2,200 to replace. Read the fine print before you need it.

4. Skip the policies that sound impressive but do not fit a one-person business

Insurance sellers love to upsell. A one-person freelancer usually does not need workers' compensation (you have no employees), commercial auto insurance (your personal car policy usually covers occasional business driving — check it), or business interruption insurance (your "business" is you, and a policy that pays your salary while you recover is usually health or disability insurance in disguise). In the sample, a freelancer who was quoted a $200-a-month package found that half the coverage was for risks she did not have — dropping the extras cut the bill to $35 a month. Buy the risks you actually have.

5. Get a quote from three places before you buy

Insurance prices for the same coverage vary wildly between providers, and freelancers are a small, low-risk category that some insurers price well and others price badly. Get quotes from at least three sources: a freelancer-focused insurer, a general online business insurer, and a local broker. Compare the same limits and the same deductible — a cheaper quote with a $5,000 deductible is not cheaper than a pricier quote with a $500 deductible. In the sample, a freelancer who compared three quotes for the same $1M professional liability policy found prices from $28 to $61 a month. The coverage was identical; only the price changed.

6. Check your contracts — some clients require coverage before you start

More and more client contracts include an insurance clause, and it is usually non-negotiable: carry the coverage, or do not get the work. Before you sign, read the insurance section of every contract and note the required limits — commonly $1M per occurrence for professional liability. If you already have a policy, check that your limits meet the requirement; if they do not, raising them is usually a small price change. In the sample, a freelancer who landed a retainer client discovered the contract required $1M in coverage — raising her existing $500K policy to $1M cost her $6 more a month, and the retainer was worth far more. Read the clause first, sign second.

7. Budget the premium like any other business cost — and review it yearly

Insurance is a business expense, not an emergency. Put the monthly premium in your budget next to software and subscriptions, and review the coverage once a year: has your income grown, have you taken on bigger clients, have you started working on-site? If the answer is yes to any of these, your coverage probably needs to grow too. In the sample, a freelancer who reviewed her policy each January found her $35-a-month premium still fit after two years of growth — because her client mix had not changed. A yearly 15-minute review is all it takes to stay covered.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer insurance checklist

  1. Buy professional liability first — it is the policy clients ask for by name.
  2. Add general liability only if you meet clients in person or work on-site.
  3. Check whether your home or renter's policy covers your business equipment — and close the gap if it does not.
  4. Skip workers' comp, commercial auto, and business interruption unless you actually have those risks.
  5. Get three quotes for the same limits and the same deductible before you buy.
  6. Read the insurance clause in every client contract before you sign.
  7. Put the premium in your monthly budget and review your coverage once a year.
