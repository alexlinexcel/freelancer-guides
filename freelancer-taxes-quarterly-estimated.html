Freelancer Taxes: Quarterly Estimated Tax, Made Simple

By Alex Lin · November 15, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Nobody becomes a freelancer for the tax paperwork, but the tax bill arrives anyway — and for freelancers it arrives four times a year, not once. When you work for yourself, no employer withholds tax from your pay, so the tax office expects you to send estimated payments every quarter. Miss them and you can owe penalties on top of the tax itself. The good news: the system is simpler than it looks. Here is how quarterly estimated tax works, plus a 7-step checklist to set it up in one afternoon.

1. Understand what "estimated" actually means

Estimated tax is not a guess and not an extra tax. It is the same income tax and self-employment tax you would owe at year end, paid in four instalments during the year instead of one lump sum. In the sample, a freelancer earning $48,000 sample annual income sets aside 25% of every payment — $12,000 sample a year, or $3,000 sample a quarter. The percentage is the estimate; the final bill at tax time settles the difference. You are not paying more tax than an employee. You are paying the same tax on a different schedule.

2. Set aside tax the day money arrives

The single habit that prevents tax panic is splitting every payment on arrival: tax first, then savings, then spending. In the sample, a freelancer who moved 25% of each invoice into a separate tax account the day it was paid never missed a quarterly payment, while a colleague who left everything in one account spent the tax money on a slow month and owed $3,000 sample he no longer had. Do not wait until the quarter ends to find the money. Move it the day it lands, when you still have it.

3. Know the four due dates

Quarterly payments have fixed deadlines, and they are not evenly spaced — the first quarter is only three months, the second is two. In the sample, a freelancer wrote the 4 sample due dates on her calendar at the start of the year (mid-April, mid-June, mid-September, mid-January) and set a reminder one week before each, so no deadline ever surprised her. Check your own tax authority's dates — they change slightly by year and country — and put them somewhere you will actually see them. A missed deadline is the most common way freelancers pay penalties.

4. Track income and expenses every month

Your estimate is only as good as your records. Keep a running log of every invoice paid and every business expense, month by month, so the quarterly amount is a calculation, not a guess. In the sample, a freelancer who logged income weekly found her fourth-quarter estimate was $800 sample lower than her first — her income had dipped, and her records caught it in time. Without records you pay based on last year, which is the wrong year. Log it monthly and the estimate follows the real numbers.

5. Deduct what the rules allow — and keep proof

Freelancers can usually deduct real business costs: software, home office, equipment, professional fees, part of phone and internet. Every deduction lowers taxable income, so tracking expenses is worth money, not just tidiness. In the sample, a freelancer who logged $6,000 sample of business expenses across the year cut her taxable income by that amount — but only because she kept receipts and a simple expense log for every item. A deduction without proof is a deduction you cannot defend. Keep the receipt, keep the log.

6. Pay online and keep the confirmation

Quarterly payments are easiest online, straight from your bank account, and the confirmation is your proof of payment. In the sample, a freelancer who paid each quarter online and saved the confirmation number in her tax folder sorted out a "missing payment" letter in ten minutes — the confirmation proved she had paid. Paper cheques get lost, bank statements get buried, but a saved confirmation settles the question. Pay online, save the receipt, move on.

7. Reconcile at year end — and adjust next year

After the final quarter, compare what you paid with what you actually owed. Paid too much? The extra comes back as a refund or a credit. Paid too little? You settle the difference — and you adjust next year's percentage so it does not happen again. In the sample, a freelancer who owed $900 sample extra at year end raised her set-aside from 25% to 27% the next year and never owed a balance again. Estimated tax is a loop, not a one-off: pay, reconcile, adjust, repeat.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer quarterly tax checklist

  1. Work out your set-aside rate — 25% is a common starting point; adjust to your bracket.
  2. Open a separate tax account and move the set-aside the day each payment arrives.
  3. Write down the 4 quarterly due dates for your country and set a reminder one week before each.
  4. Log income and expenses monthly, so each quarter's amount comes from real numbers.
  5. Keep receipts for every business deduction, with a one-line note on what each was for.
  6. Pay each quarter online and save the confirmation number in your tax folder.
  7. At year end, reconcile paid vs owed, then adjust next year's rate if the gap was large.
