Freelancer Agency Work: How to Win Subcontracted Gigs (and Keep Them Coming)

By Alex Lin · November 16, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Direct clients are not the only way to fill a calendar. Agencies — design studios, marketing firms, web shops — constantly have more work than their in-house team can handle, and they quietly hand the overflow to freelancers. Agency work pays differently from direct work: the rates are often lower per hour, but the pipeline is steadier, the briefs arrive pre-sold, and you never have to chase the client yourself. For many freelancers it becomes the reliable floor under a lumpy income. Here are seven moves to win agency gigs and keep them coming.

1. Pitch the agency, not the agency's client

Agencies do not hire you to impress their clients; they hire you to make their life easy. Your pitch should answer one question: can this person take a brief and return finished work without hand-holding? Lead with reliability, not creativity — turnaround time, file hygiene, and the ability to follow a house style. In the sample, a designer who pitched ten agencies led with "48-hour turnaround on overflow work, files delivered in your templates" and heard back from four; her portfolio link was the second paragraph, not the first. Agencies buy a safe pair of hands first and a portfolio second.

2. Be easy to brief — and easier to pay

The fastest way to become an agency's first call is to remove friction. Ask how they like files delivered, learn their naming conventions, and match their invoice format. Send one clean invoice per job, on time, with the project name they use internally. In the sample, a freelancer who started labelling invoices with the agency's job codes — instead of her own descriptions — went from "we will get to it" to payment in seven days, because her invoices stopped needing a translation step. Agencies remember the freelancer whose paperwork never causes a meeting.

3. Say yes to the boring overflow first

The first jobs an agency sends will not be glamorous: resizing banners, formatting reports, fixing someone else's spreadsheet. Take them, do them well, and do them fast. Overflow work is a test, not the job itself — agencies promote the reliable freelancer to the interesting work once trust is built. In the sample, a freelancer who spent three months on "boring" overflow formatting was then handed a full client rebrand, because she was the only subcontractor who had never missed a deadline. The boring work is the audition.

4. Protect your rate — agency work still has a floor

Agencies will sometimes ask for a discount "because we send volume". Volume is real, but only if it actually arrives. Agree a rate for the first few jobs, and revisit it once the volume is proven — not before. A sample floor: if an agency wants 20% off your direct rate, ask for a monthly minimum of hours in return. In the sample, a freelancer who accepted a lower agency rate without a minimum spent two months waiting for work that never came, at a rate she had already discounted. A discount without a commitment is just a lower rate.

5. Never pitch the agency's client — that is the one unforgivable rule

If an agency introduces you to their client, even by accident, you work for the agency on that job, not for the client. Do not hand over your card, do not offer a cheaper direct rate, and do not take the client's next project behind the agency's back. Agencies talk to each other, and one poached client ends more relationships than any missed deadline. In the sample, a freelancer who was offered direct work by an agency's client told the agency first and let them decide; the agency kept her on retainer for two years after that. Loyalty is the product agencies are actually buying.

6. Keep two agencies warm, not one

One agency can go quiet without warning — a lost client, a hiring freeze, a new in-house hire. Two agencies rarely go quiet at the same time. Keep a light touch with a second agency even when the first is busy: a monthly check-in, a sample of recent work, a note that you have capacity next month. In the sample, a freelancer who replaced 40% of her direct-client income with two agency retainers survived one agency going dark for a quarter, because the other one doubled her hours within a week. Two warm relationships are the cheapest insurance in this business.

7. Track agency hours separately — they behave differently

Agency work has its own patterns: rush jobs, revision rounds you did not cause, and hours that bunch at month-end. Log agency hours in a separate bucket in your time tracker, and review them monthly. In the sample, a freelancer who tracked agency work separately discovered her agency effective rate was $60 an hour on paper but $44 after unpaid revision rounds — so she renegotiated revision limits on the next retainer and the effective rate recovered. Numbers you do not separate are numbers you cannot negotiate with.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer agency-work checklist

  1. Pitch agencies on reliability first — turnaround, file hygiene, house style — portfolio second.
  2. Match the agency's file naming and invoice format so your paperwork never causes a meeting.
  3. Say yes to boring overflow work first — it is the audition for the interesting jobs.
  4. Set a rate floor: any discount needs a monthly hours minimum in return.
  5. Never pitch an agency's client — loyalty is the product agencies are buying.
  6. Keep two agencies warm with a monthly check-in, so one going quiet never empties your calendar.
  7. Track agency hours in a separate bucket and review the effective rate monthly.
