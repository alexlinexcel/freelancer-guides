Freelancer Kill Fees: How to Get Paid When a Client Cancels Mid-Project

By Alex Lin · November 17, 2026 · Freelancing guides

A client cancels. The budget vanished, the manager changed, the project no longer fits the quarter — none of it is your fault, and all of it can leave you unpaid for work already done. In the sample, a $3,000 website project is cancelled when it is 70% complete, and the freelancer with a kill fee in the agreement is still paid for the work; the freelancer without one is paid nothing for two weeks of building, because the contract only promised payment on delivery. A kill fee is the price of cancellation, agreed in advance, when everyone is still friendly. It does not make cancellations pleasant. It makes them survivable — and it makes the client think twice before cancelling over something small.

1. What a kill fee actually is — and what it is not

A kill fee is a fixed payment the client owes if they cancel the project after work has started. It is not a penalty and it is not a punishment. It is compensation for two things the client cannot give back: the work you already did, and the other work you turned away to keep their slot open. That is why it is agreed before the project starts, in writing, with a number both sides can see. It is not a surprise charge invented on cancellation day, and it is not a threat to wave at a client who is already upset. Think of it as the cancellation terms of the deal, the same way a hotel has a cancellation policy: nobody reads it hoping to use it, and everybody is glad it is there when plans change. The freelancer who explains it that way — calmly, at the start — almost never has to argue about it later.

2. How much to charge: the 50% rule and when to bend it

For most projects, start with 50% of the remaining fee, on top of the deposit already paid. In the sample, a $3,000 project cancelled at 70% complete leaves $1,500 of the fee unpaid at that point in the schedule — the kill fee is 50% of that remainder, so the freelancer keeps the deposit and collects the kill fee on top of it. The percentage is the point: it is high enough to matter, low enough that a reasonable client signs it without a fight. In the sample, 3 of 4 clients who were told the kill fee up front accepted it without negotiating, and the fourth asked for the percentage to drop from 50% to 25%. Dropping to 25% is fine if the deposit is already large — the deposit plus a smaller kill fee still covers the work done. What never works is a kill fee of zero with a promise to "sort it out later". Later is exactly when the client has no reason to be generous. Agree the number when everyone is friendly. That is the only moment it is easy.

3. The three moments a project dies — and what each one pays

Cancellations are not all the same, and the kill fee should say what each one costs. First, cancellation before any work starts: in the sample, a $2,000 project is cancelled after the deposit is paid but before any work starts, and the deposit of $1,000 is kept in full — the deposit was the price of holding the slot, and the slot is gone. Second, cancellation mid-project: the deposit plus the kill fee on the remaining fee, which is the case most freelancers actually face. Third, cancellation after the work is delivered but before approval: the full fee, because the work exists and the client has it. Write all three into the agreement as one short paragraph. Clients accept it easily because it is obviously fair — the further the work has gone, the more of the fee is owed — and you never have to negotiate the price of a cancellation while you are also processing the disappointment of losing the project.

4. Write it in one paragraph, before the work starts

A kill fee that lives in an email thread is a misunderstanding waiting to happen. Put it in the agreement, in plain language, in one paragraph: what happens if either side cancels, how much is owed at each stage, and when the final invoice is due. No legal jargon is needed. "If the client cancels after work has started, the client pays the deposit plus 50% of the remaining fee, invoiced within 24 hours of the cancellation and due within 14 days" is a complete kill-fee clause. Both sides sign it before the first hour of work. The client reads it once, nods, and never thinks about it again — until the day a cancellation happens, when they find the number already there, already agreed. That is the real value of the clause: it takes the money conversation out of the worst day of the project entirely.

5. The cancellation call: stay calm, then invoice within 24 hours

Sooner or later the call comes, and the first five minutes matter more than the clause. Stay calm, thank the client for telling you directly, and ask one question: is this a pause or a cancellation? A pause keeps the project alive and the kill fee untriggered; a cancellation triggers it. Do not negotiate the fee on the call — say the agreement covers it and you will send the final invoice today. Then do exactly that. In the sample, the kill-fee invoice is always sent within 24 hours of the cancellation, while the details are still fresh for both sides. The invoice lists the deposit already paid, the work completed, and the kill fee as its own line, so the client sees the arithmetic instead of a single unexplained number. In the sample, one cancelled project was paid in full within 9 days of the kill-fee invoice, and one took 26 days and one reminder. Speed is not pushiness. It is the difference between a clean ending and a debt that drags on for months.

6. When the client pushes back — and when to hold the line

Some clients will push back on the kill fee, and most of the time it is not a fight — it is a misunderstanding. The usual objection is "but we didn't get anything". The answer is in the invoice: the hours logged, the drafts delivered, the slot held. In the sample, a $4,800 project is cancelled two weeks in, the freelancer has logged 18 hours, and the final invoice is the deposit plus 50% of the remaining fee — the logged hours are the evidence, and the clause is the authority. If the client genuinely cannot pay, offer a split: half now, half in 30 days, with the same total. What you do not do is waive the fee to "keep the relationship". A client who cancels and pays the kill fee is a client who respects your business; a client who cancels and pays nothing is a client who has learned your time is free. Hold the line once, politely, and the next project with that client — or the next client they refer — starts on better terms.

7. After the kill fee: close the project cleanly

Getting paid is only half the ending. Close the project the way you would close a finished one: hand over whatever work exists, in the state it is in, with a short note on where it stops. Confirm in writing that the kill fee settles the project in full and that neither side owes the other anything further. Then file it away — the agreement, the invoice, the payment confirmation — and move on. The freelancers who recover fastest from a cancellation are the ones who treat it as a closed chapter the same week it happens, not as a story they retell for months. And the kill fee is what makes that possible: it turns a lost project into a paid one, a gap in the calendar into a gap you can afford, and a client who cancelled into a client you would still work with again.
