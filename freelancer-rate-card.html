Freelancer Rate Card: How to Publish Your Prices (and Stop Negotiating Every Project)

By Alex Lin · November 16, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Most freelancers treat their prices like a secret. A client asks "what do you charge?", and the freelancer writes a careful email, waits, gets asked "can you do it for less?", writes another careful email, and by the time a number is agreed, a week has passed and the project has already started to feel like a chore. The alternative is a rate card: a short, public list of what you offer and what it costs. In the sample, a freelancer who published a three-package rate card went from 14 pricing emails a month to 3 — the other 11 enquiries either booked straight from the card or quietly walked away, and both outcomes saved time. A rate card does not lock you into one price forever. It locks you out of the negotiation that wastes your week. This guide shows how to build one in seven steps.

1. Decide what the card is for: filter, not catalogue

A rate card is not a menu of everything you have ever done. It is a filter that answers one question for the client — "can I afford this person?" — before either of you spends an hour on a call. In the sample, the freelancer's card listed three packages and nothing else: a $150 starter, a $450 standard, and a $1,200 full package. Everything the freelancer could do was not on the card; only the three things most clients actually buy were. The card's job is to let the wrong clients leave quietly and the right ones arrive already knowing the price. In the sample, about 20% of enquiries self-selected out on price before any call — clients who would have negotiated the freelancer down to a number nobody was happy with. That is not lost work. That is a week of unpaid negotiation handed back. If your card tries to cover every service, every edge case, and every possible client, it is not a filter. It is a catalogue, and nobody reads catalogues. Three offers, one page, one job: tell the client the price before the conversation starts.

2. Pick three packages: good, better, best — with names, not numbers

Three is the number that works. One package gives the client nothing to compare against; five gives them a spreadsheet to agonise over. Three packages — good, better, best — let the client pick a level instead of haggling over a rate. Give each package a name that says what it is, not a number: "Starter", "Standard", "Full" beats "Package 1, 2, 3" because the client can picture themselves in one. In the sample, the $150 starter was a single deliverable with one revision, the $450 standard was the same work with two revisions and a faster turnaround, and the $1,200 full package added the extras clients kept asking for — a call, a handover note, and priority scheduling. The middle package is the one most clients choose, so make it the one you most want to sell: put your best value there, and let the top package exist so the middle one looks reasonable. The bottom package exists so the client who cannot afford you yet still has a door in — and so the client who can afford more does not mistake the bottom for the whole of your work. Three packages, named, priced, and honest about what each one includes.

3. Price from your costs, not from your fear

The hardest part of a rate card is writing the numbers down, because the numbers feel like a judgement on your worth. They are not. They are arithmetic. Start from what a month of your business costs — rent, software, tax set aside, the income you need to live — and divide by the billable hours you can actually sell. In the sample, the freelancer's floor was $60 an hour once costs and tax were counted; the $450 standard package was priced from 6 hours of work plus a margin, not from a guess about what clients might pay. Then check the market once, briefly: what do three freelancers at your level charge for the same work? If your number is far below theirs, the card is not cheap — it is a signal that you do not believe your own work. Price the card from costs first and the market second, and never from the fear that nobody will book. The clients who leave because of the price were never going to pay what the work costs, and the ones who stay are the ones you can afford to keep.

4. Say what is included — and what is not

A price without a scope is an invitation to argue. Every package on the card needs two short lists: what is included, and what costs extra. In the sample, the standard package listed "one deliverable, two revisions, delivery in 5 working days" and, underneath, "extra revisions $75 each, rush delivery +50%". That second line is the one that saves the relationship: when a client asks for a third revision, the answer is already written down, and nobody has to feel awkward about it. Keep the lists short — three to five items each. If the included list runs to ten items, the package is really two packages hiding in one. And be specific about the thing clients most often misunderstand: revisions, turnaround time, and who supplies what. "Two revisions" means two rounds of changes, not two changed words. Write it the way you would explain it to a friend, and the card stops being a price list and starts being a contract in plain language.

5. Put the card where clients actually look

A rate card hidden in a PDF nobody opens is a secret with extra steps. Put it where the pricing question already gets asked: on your website's services page, at the bottom of your proposal, and in your profile on the platforms where clients find you. In the sample, the freelancer put the three packages on a single page linked from the homepage — "Rates" in the top menu, no hunting — and pasted the same three lines at the bottom of every proposal, so a client who skipped the website still saw the numbers before the call. The card should take less than a minute to read: three packages, three prices, a line each on what is included, and a button that says "book a call" or "start a project". If the client has to download something to see your prices, you have already lost the clients who were ready to book. One page, always current, always in the same place. When you change a price, change it in all three places the same day, or the card starts lying to someone.

6. Handle the "can you do it cheaper?" email

Publishing prices does not stop the discount question — it just makes the answer easy. In the sample, the freelancer still got the "can you do it cheaper?" email, about 3 times a month instead of 14, and the reply was one sentence: "The starter package is the smallest version of this work — happy to do that one if the budget is tight." No apology, no long justification, no invented discount. The card gives you the sentence, because the negotiation is no longer about your worth — it is about which package fits. If a client genuinely cannot afford the smallest package, say so kindly and move on; a project taken at a price that does not cover your costs is a project that teaches the client your work is cheap. And keep one rule for yourself: never discount the card in the first email. If you discount on request, the card is not a price list — it is an opening bid, and every client will learn to open lower. The card works because the price on it is the price. Protect that, and the card protects you.

7. Review the card every 3 months — and raise it in public

A rate card is not a tombstone. Review it every 3 months: which package sells most, which one nobody buys, and whether the hours behind each price still match reality. In the sample, the freelancer's time tracker showed the $450 standard package taking 7 hours instead of 6, so the price went to $520 at the next review — and the card was updated the same day, in all three places, with a short note to existing clients: "rates updated from March 1". Raising prices in public feels scarier than raising them in private, but it is also fairer: the client sees the same number everyone else sees, and there is no quiet deal for one client and a different one for another. In the sample, the freelancer raised the card twice in a year, about 10% each time, and lost no repeat clients — because the clients who stayed were the ones who had chosen the work, not the discount. A rate card that never changes is a freelancer who never grows. Review it, raise it, and let the card do the talking.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer rate card checklist

  1. Decide what the card is for: a filter, not a catalogue — in the sample, three packages only, and about 20% of enquiries self-selected out on price before any call.
  2. Pick three packages: good, better, best — in the sample, $150 starter, $450 standard, $1,200 full — with names, not numbers, and the best value in the middle.
  3. Price from your costs, not from your fear: in the sample, the floor was $60 an hour once costs and tax were counted, and the $450 package was priced from 6 hours of work plus a margin.
  4. Say what is included — and what is not: three to five items each, including revisions, turnaround time, and the price of extras (in the sample, extra revisions $75 each, rush delivery +50%).
  5. Put the card where clients actually look: your website's services page, the bottom of every proposal, and your platform profiles — readable in under a minute, no download needed.
  6. Handle the "can you do it cheaper?" email: in the sample, the question fell from 14 times a month to 3 — answer with the starter package, one sentence, and never discount the card in the first email.
  7. Review the card every 3 months: in the sample, the standard package took 7 hours instead of 6, so the price went to $520 — raise it in public, about 10% each time, and update all three places the same day.
