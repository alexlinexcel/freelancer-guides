Freelancer Upfront Payment: How to Get Paid Before You Start Work (Without Scaring Clients Away)

By Alex Lin · November 17, 2026 · Freelancing guides

The freelancer's worst invoice is the one that never gets paid. In the sample, a freelancer finished a $1,800 project, sent the invoice, and heard nothing for three months — then the client replied that the budget had been cut and the work was "no longer needed". That freelancer had 2 unpaid invoices in one year, totalling $2,400 of work done for free. The fix was not better reminders or firmer late fees. The fix was money before work: a 50% upfront payment of $900 on every new project, before a single hour was billed. After the change, the same freelancer had 0 unpaid invoices — not because clients became more honest, but because the clients who would not pay upfront never became clients at all. Upfront payment is not rude, and it is not a sign you do not trust the client. It is the normal way every other business works: the plumber takes a deposit, the hotel takes your card, the shop takes payment before the goods leave the shelf. This guide shows how to ask for it in seven steps.

1. Upfront payment is normal — say so, and mean it

Most freelancers apologise for asking for money upfront, as if the request itself were an insult. It is not. Every trade that sells custom work takes payment before delivery, because custom work cannot be returned to the shelf. The plumber orders parts with your deposit. The printer buys paper with it. You are doing the same thing: reserving a week of your calendar that you cannot sell to anyone else. In the sample, the freelancer stopped apologising and started treating the deposit as a line item, not a negotiation: "50% upfront to book the project, 50% on delivery." No explanation, no justification, no "I hope that's okay". Clients did not flinch, because the sentence sounded like a process, not a plea. The ones who pushed back were not offended — they were testing whether the rule was real. A freelancer who asks for payment upfront is not suspicious of the client. A freelancer who never asks is the one clients quietly worry about, because a business that does not protect itself rarely protects anyone else either. State the terms plainly, early, and as if they were always there. They should be.

2. How much to ask: the 50% rule, and when to change it

Fifty percent is the default for a reason: it is big enough to hurt if the client walks away, and small enough that a serious client can say yes without a meeting. In the sample, the $1,800 project split into $900 upfront and $900 on delivery — the freelancer was never more than half unpaid, and the client was never asked for the whole fee before seeing anything. Change the number when the job changes shape. For small jobs under $300, ask for 100% upfront or a 25% non-refundable booking fee — the admin cost of chasing a $150 balance is worth more than the balance itself. For large jobs over $5,000, 50% upfront can feel steep to a client who has never worked with you; split it into milestones instead (step 3). For repeat clients with a clean payment history, you can drop to 30% or even bill on delivery — trust, once earned, is a discount you can afford to give. For brand-new clients, never drop below 50% on a first project, no matter how friendly the first call felt. The percentage is not about the money. It is about the commitment: a client who has paid half has already decided this project is happening, and a freelancer who has been paid half can afford to start.

3. Split big projects into milestones — and tie payment to delivery, not dates

A single 50% deposit works up to a point. On a three-month project, the second half of the fee sits unpaid for twelve weeks, and the client has no reason to pay it until the very end. Milestones fix that. In the sample, a $6,000 project split into 50/30/20: $3,000 upfront, $1,800 when the first draft was delivered, $1,200 when the final files were handed over. Each payment was tied to something the client could see — a draft, a delivery, a handover — not to a calendar date. Dates slip; deliverables do not. Two rules make milestones work. First, never start the next phase until the previous milestone is paid — the moment you work ahead of payment, the milestone stops being a milestone and becomes a suggestion. Second, write the milestones into the proposal in plain words: what is delivered, what it costs, and what happens next. In the sample, the freelancer's proposal listed each milestone on one line — "Milestone 1: first draft, $1,800, due on delivery" — and the client paid each one within two days of delivery, because the invoice arrived with the work attached, not a month later. Milestones turn one big risk into three small ones, and three small risks are easy to carry.

4. Put it in writing before work starts — proposal, invoice, and a date

Upfront payment agreed in a call is a promise. Upfront payment written in the proposal is a term. The difference shows up the first time a client is late. In the sample, the freelancer's proposal carried three lines, every time: the deposit amount, the payment method, and the date — "50% upfront ($900), by bank transfer, within 7 days of this proposal, before work starts on March 3." The invoice went out the same day the proposal was accepted, not the day work started, so the 7-day window was real and the start date was protected. If the payment did not arrive by the date, the start date moved — not as a punishment, but as a fact: "Work starts once the deposit clears, so the delivery date moves with it." That sentence, in writing, ended the awkward chasing entirely, because there was nothing left to negotiate. The client knew the rule, the freelancer knew the rule, and the calendar enforced it for both of them. Never start work on a verbal yes. The proposal is where the money is agreed, the invoice is where it is collected, and the start date is where it is enforced. All three, in writing, before the first hour.

5. The exact words to ask for it

Freelancers do not lose deposits because clients refuse them. They lose them because they never ask clearly. Use the same words every time, so the ask becomes a habit instead of a performance. In the proposal, one line: "Payment: 50% upfront ($900) to book the project, 50% on delivery of the final files." In the email that sends the invoice, two sentences: "Here's the invoice for the 50% deposit — $900. Once it clears, I'll start on March 3 and the first draft will be with you by March 10." On a call, if the client asks why, one sentence: "It books your slot in my calendar and covers the first half of the work — it's how I run every project." That is the whole script. No apology, no long story about the client who never paid, no "I hope you understand". In the sample, the freelancer used those exact lines for a year and had 3 clients push back — 2 walked away, and 1 paid after seeing the terms in writing in the proposal. The 2 who walked away were the point: the deposit had done its job before any work was done. If you find yourself writing a paragraph to justify the deposit, stop. One line in the proposal, two sentences in the email, one sentence on the call. The shorter the ask, the more professional it sounds.

6. What to do when a client says no

A client who refuses upfront payment has told you something useful, and your job is to hear it rather than argue with it. First, find out what the no means. A big company with a 30-day payment policy is not refusing — it is following a process, and the answer is a smaller first milestone or a paid discovery phase: a $300 paid test, delivered in two days, that proves the working relationship before the big invoice. A small business that genuinely cannot pay half upfront may accept a 25% booking fee plus a milestone at first delivery — less protection, but a real commitment. A client who refuses any payment before delivery, on any terms, is a client who wants you to carry all the risk. In the sample, the freelancer's rule was simple: no deposit, no start date, no exceptions — and the 2 clients who walked away over it were replaced within a month by clients who paid upfront without a word. The test is not whether the client is a good person. The test is whether they will share the risk of a project they asked you to build. If the answer is no, the project was never yours to fund. Offer the smaller milestone once, in writing, and then let the client decide. A no that costs you nothing is the cheapest no you will ever get.

7. What upfront payment does to your cash flow

The real prize of upfront payment is not protection. It is cash flow. In the sample, the freelancer's budget planner showed the change clearly: before upfront payment, income arrived in lumps — nothing for six weeks, then three invoices at once, then another dry month. After it, every project paid half at the start, so there was always money coming in while work was underway. The sample numbers: 2 unpaid invoices totalling $2,400 in the year before, 0 unpaid in the year after — and about 4 hours a month saved, the hours that used to go to reminder emails and awkward follow-up calls. The deposit also changed which projects the freelancer took: with half the fee in hand, a slow-paying client was an annoyance, not a crisis, and the freelancer could afford to say no to the next bad project instead of taking it out of fear. Review your terms once a year, not once a project: is 50% still right, do milestones need to move, is the booking fee for small jobs still worth it? Upfront payment is not a trick to squeeze clients. It is the difference between a business that gets paid for its work and a hobby that hopes it will be. Put the money first, and the work gets easier, not harder.

  4. Put it in writing before work starts: proposal, invoice, and a date — in the sample, "50% upfront ($900), by bank transfer, within 7 days of this proposal, before work starts on March 3"; if payment is late, the start date moves with it.
  5. Use the same words every time: one line in the proposal, two sentences in the email, one sentence on the call — "It books your slot in my calendar and covers the first half of the work — it's how I run every project."

  7. Watch your cash flow change: in the sample, 2 unpaid invoices totalling $2,400 in the year before upfront payment, 0 unpaid in the year after, and about 4 hours a month saved not chasing invoices — review the terms once a year.
