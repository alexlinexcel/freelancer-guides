Freelancer Client Questionnaire: The Questions to Ask Before You Quote (and the Ones That Save You From Bad Projects)

By Alex Lin · November 17, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Every freelancer knows the call that goes nowhere. Forty-five minutes on the phone, a client who talks in circles, and at the end of it you still do not know what they want, when they want it, or what they can pay. So you write the proposal anyway, guessing at all three — and then you spend a week revising it, or you win the project and discover the real job is twice the size of the one you quoted. A client questionnaire fixes this before it starts. It is a short written form you send before the first call: eight questions, five minutes of the client's time, and by the time you talk, you already know the goal, the deliverables, the deadline, the budget range, and who decides. This guide is the questionnaire itself — the questions to ask, the answers that warn you off, and what to do with the answers once you have them.

1. Why a questionnaire beats a call that wanders — get it in writing first

A first call feels productive and usually is not. People talk around the point, forget the details, and remember a different version of the conversation than you do. A questionnaire does three things a call cannot. First, it makes the client think before they talk: writing "redesign the checkout page so fewer people abandon their carts" is a clearer brief than twenty minutes of "we need something fresher." Second, it gives you a written record — when the scope drifts later, you can point at the client's own words instead of arguing about what was said (article 38 covers scope creep, and this is where it starts). Third, it screens. A client who will not spend four minutes answering eight questions will not spend four weeks being a good client either. In the sample, 8 of 10 clients who filled in the questionnaire before the first call signed within a week, against 3 of 10 who went straight to a call with no written brief — not because the form is magic, but because the clients who fill it in are the clients who know what they want. Article 100 covers the interview itself; the questionnaire is what makes the interview worth having.

2. The six questions every questionnaire needs — goal, deliverables, deadline, budget, decider, materials

You do not need twenty questions. You need six, and they are always the same. One: what is the goal — what should be different when this project is done? Two: what exactly do you need delivered — the list, not a vibe. Three: when does it have to be finished, and is that date fixed? Four: what budget range are you working in? Five: who makes the final decision, and are they the person filling in this form? Six: what materials do you already have — files, logins, brand assets, existing copy? Those six answers are the whole project in miniature. Everything else is detail you can collect later. Ask them in plain language, one line each, with a text box big enough for a real answer and no question that can be answered "yes." A client who writes two sentences per question has given you a brief. A client who writes two words per question has told you something too — just not what they think they have.

3. The budget question, without the awkwardness — ask for a range, not a number

Most freelancers skip the budget question because it feels rude, and then they quote blind. Ask for a range instead of a number and the awkwardness disappears. "Roughly what range have you budgeted for this — under $500, $500–$1,500, $1,500–$5,000, over $5,000?" Nobody has to name a figure, and you still learn whether you are in the same universe. A client who ticks "under $500" for a project you would price at $3,000 is not a bad client — they are a mismatch, and you have found it out in four minutes instead of four hours of proposal writing. In the sample, a freelancer who added one budget-range question cut unpaid quoting time from 6 hours a week to 2, because the mismatches were spotted before the proposal was written. If the client skips the question entirely, that is also an answer: note it, and raise the budget on the call before you quote anything. 4. Red-flag answers — and what each one really means

Some answers are not information. They are warnings. "ASAP" as the deadline, with no date attached, usually means the client has no plan and will blame you for the rush (article 111 covers rush fees — a hidden rush is the expensive kind). "We don't have a budget yet" from a client who also has a fixed launch date usually means the budget will appear after you have done the work. "Just make it pop" or "I'll know it when I see it" as the goal usually means endless revisions, because the target does not exist yet. "My boss decides, but I'm the contact" usually means you will present to someone who never read the brief. "We had three freelancers before you" usually means the problem is the client, not the freelancers — ask what happened, and listen to whether the answer blames everyone else. In the sample, 5 of 7 red-flag answers that were ignored anyway turned into scope creep or a late payment, against 1 of 9 projects where the flag was acted on. A red flag is not a refusal. It is a question to ask on the call — and a reason to put the answer in the contract (article 16) before you start.

5. Keep it short — eight questions, five minutes, or nobody finishes it

The most common questionnaire mistake is making it long. Twelve questions, three pages, a question about the client's company history — and the client closes the tab. In the sample, clients took 4 minutes on average to finish an 8-question form, and the completion rate fell from 9 in 10 to 4 in 10 when the form grew past 12 questions. Eight is the ceiling, not the floor: the six from section 2, plus one about how they found you, plus one open box — "anything else I should know?" — so the client can tell you the thing you forgot to ask. Make every question optional except the goal and the deadline, so a busy client can still finish. Send it as a simple form, not a document to download, fill in, and email back — every extra step loses a client. And send it the moment the enquiry arrives, while the client is still thinking about the project. A questionnaire sent three days later is a questionnaire answered by someone who has already talked to your competitor.

6. What to do with the answers — scope, quote, and the call agenda

The answers are not a filing exercise. They are the proposal. Read them once and write three things before the call: the scope in one paragraph, the price or price range, and the three questions the answers did not settle. In the sample, a designer who quoted from questionnaire answers instead of from a call finished proposals in 25 minutes instead of 90, and her close rate rose from 4 in 10 to 6 in 10 — because the proposal answered the brief the client had already written, instead of the conversation she half-remembered. Use the call to test the answers, not to collect them: "You said the deadline is March 1 — is that the launch, or the date you need the files?" If an answer worries you, raise it on the call, in plain words, before you quote. And file the questionnaire with the project: when the client asks for "one small extra" in week three, the scope is the one they wrote themselves (article 17 covers onboarding, and the questionnaire is the first page of the file).

7. The template — copy this, send it, and stop quoting blind

Here is the whole questionnaire, ready to copy into any form tool. Question 1: What should be different when this project is done? Question 2: What exactly do you need delivered? List everything. Question 3: When does it have to be finished, and is that date fixed? Question 4: Roughly what range have you budgeted — under $500, $500–$1,500, $1,500–$5,000, over $5,000? Question 5: Who makes the final decision — is that you? Question 6: What materials do you already have — files, logins, brand assets, existing copy? Question 7: How did you find me? Question 8: Anything else I should know? Send it with one line: "Before we talk, could you fill this in — eight questions, about four minutes — so I can come to the call with a real answer instead of guesses?" Article 59 covers the email wording; the form does the rest. The clients who fill it in are telling you they are serious. The clients who do not are telling you something too. Either way, you stop quoting blind — and the next proposal you write is built on the client's own words, not on a forty-five-minute call you are still trying to remember.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer client-questionnaire checklist

  1. Send the questionnaire before the first call, not after — in the sample, 8 of 10 clients who filled it in before the call signed within a week, against 3 of 10 who went straight to a call with no written brief.
  2. Ask the six core questions every time — goal, deliverables, deadline, budget range, decision-maker, materials — plus how they found you and one open box; eight questions is the ceiling.
  3. Ask for a budget range, not a number — in the sample, one range question cut unpaid quoting time from 6 hours a week to 2, because mismatches were spotted before the proposal was written.
  4. Read the answers as warnings, not just information — in the sample, 5 of 7 ignored red-flag answers turned into scope creep or late payment, against 1 of 9 where the flag was acted on.
  5. Keep the form under five minutes — in the sample, clients averaged 4 minutes on 8 questions, and completion fell from 9 in 10 to 4 in 10 past 12 questions; make only the goal and the deadline required.
  6. Write the scope, price range, and three open questions from the answers before the call — in the sample, quoting from the answers cut proposal time from 90 minutes to 25 and lifted the close rate from 4 in 10 to 6 in 10.
  7. File the answered questionnaire with the project — it is the scope the client wrote themselves, and the record you point at when the extras start arriving.
