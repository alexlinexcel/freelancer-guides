Freelancer Payment Methods: How to Get Paid by Clients Abroad (Without Losing Money to Fees)

By Alex Lin · November 15, 2026 · Freelancing guides

Getting the work is only half the job. The other half is getting paid — in full, on time, and without a chunk of it disappearing into transfer fees and bad exchange rates. A client abroad adds a layer most new freelancers never plan for: the money has to cross a border, change currency, and land in your account, and every step along the way can take a cut. Here are seven things to settle before you send the invoice, so the fee you quoted is the fee you actually keep.

1. Agree the payment method before you start the work

The worst time to discuss payment is after the work is done. Put the method in the contract or the project confirmation: how the client will pay, in which currency, by which date, and who covers the transfer fees. In the sample, a freelancer who quoted $1,000 for a project and only asked about payment at delivery waited three weeks while the client figured out their own finance process — then lost $29 to a wire fee nobody had agreed to cover. One sentence in the agreement — "Payment by bank transfer or Wise, in USD, within 14 days, transfer fees paid by the client" — prevents all of it. If a client cannot use your preferred method, find out before you start, not after you invoice.

2. Bank transfer: simple for big invoices, wasteful for small ones

A direct bank transfer is the oldest method and still the most reliable for large amounts: the money goes from the client's bank to yours, with a paper trail both sides can follow. The catch is the fee structure — many banks charge a flat wire fee, often around $25–45 per transfer, no matter the amount. In the sample, a $29 wire fee on a $1,000 invoice is annoying; the same $29 on a $150 invoice eats nearly a fifth of the job. Use bank transfer for large invoices where the flat fee is a small percentage, and never for small ones. Ask your bank what an incoming international wire actually costs you, including any intermediary bank charges — the fee you see is not always the fee you pay.

3. PayPal: every client already has it, and you pay for the convenience

PayPal is the method clients reach for first, because almost every business already has an account and paying takes two minutes. The convenience has a price: a typical cross-border payment costs roughly 3–4% in fees, plus a currency conversion spread if the money changes currency on the way. In the sample, a $1,000 invoice paid through PayPal from abroad arrived as roughly $960–970 after fees and conversion — $30–40 gone before the money even reached the account. That is fine for a one-off small job where speed matters, but on regular monthly work it adds up to hundreds a year. Use PayPal when the client insists or the amount is small; for steady work, move the client to a cheaper method and keep PayPal as the backup.

4. Wise: the cheap option for regular international work

Wise (formerly TransferWise) exists for exactly this problem: it converts money at the mid-market rate — the rate you see on Google — and charges a small, transparent fee instead of hiding a margin in the exchange rate. In the sample, the same $1,000 invoice received through Wise arrived with only a few dollars in fees and almost no exchange-rate loss, a difference of $25–35 per invoice compared with a card-based wallet. The trade-off is setup: both sides need an account, and the first transfer takes a little longer while identities are verified. For a client who pays you every month, that one-time setup pays for itself in the first invoice. Ask regular international clients to switch, and offer to walk them through it once.

5. Platform escrow: protection that costs a percentage

If the work came through a freelance platform, the platform usually holds the client's money in escrow and releases it when the work is approved — which means you are protected against a client who simply never pays. The protection costs a service fee, commonly around 10–20% on platforms, and the platform controls the withdrawal timing and method. In the sample, a freelancer who billed $1,000 through a platform kept about $800–900 after the platform fee, but never once chased a late payment, because the money was already held before the work began. That trade is worth it for new clients you do not know yet; for a trusted long-term client, move off-platform only when both sides agree — and never skip the written agreement when you do.

6. Pick one currency and put it on every invoice

A freelancer who bills in three currencies spends their month doing exchange-rate maths instead of work — and the tax return becomes a puzzle. Choose one invoicing currency, usually your own, and state it on every invoice, every quote, and every contract. In the sample, a freelancer who billed US clients in USD and European clients in EUR kept converting amounts back and forth and lost track of which invoices were actually profitable after conversion; switching everything to USD made the income sheet readable at a glance. If a client prefers to pay in their currency, agree on a fixed conversion rate for that invoice and write it on the invoice itself, so neither side is surprised by the amount that lands. One currency, written down, every time.

7. Track every payment: fees, exchange rates, and due dates

The money you lose to fees is invisible unless you write it down. For every invoice, record the invoiced amount, the method used, the fee taken, the exchange rate applied, and the amount that actually arrived — plus the date it was due and the date it landed. In the sample, a freelancer who tracked a quarter of invoices discovered that one payment method was costing $120 more per quarter than another, and switched the client in a single email. The same sheet tells you instantly which invoices are overdue, so you can chase a late payment the day it slips instead of noticing a month later. Payment tracking is not bookkeeping for its own sake; it is the record that shows you where your money is leaking.

Quick setup: a 7-step freelancer payment-methods checklist

  1. Put the payment method, currency, due date, and who pays transfer fees in writing before the work starts.
  2. Use bank transfer only for large invoices — check the flat wire fee first, and never let it eat a small job.
  3. Keep PayPal as the fast backup for small or one-off jobs, and know its roughly 3–4% cross-border cost before you accept it.
  4. Move regular international clients to Wise: mid-market rate, low transparent fee, one-time setup.
  5. Use platform escrow for new clients you do not know yet, and accept the service fee as the price of not chasing payment.
  6. Bill in one currency everywhere, and write the conversion rate on the invoice if a client pays in theirs.
  7. Record every invoice's fee, exchange rate, due date, and arrival date — then switch any method that is quietly costing you money.
